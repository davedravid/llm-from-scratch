# Where the video simplifies or gets details wrong

The video is a good roadmap. These points are worth correcting, and each one will come up again
in the phase where it's relevant.

| # | What the video says | More accurate version | Phase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0a | Backprop uses "the derivative to figure out the delta, how far we are off" | The *error* is how far off we are. The derivative (gradient) says **which direction and how strongly** each weight affects that error. "Delta" combines the two. | 2 |
| 0b | A single-layer perceptron can't solve XOR; multi-layer can | Correct. The precise reason: one layer can only draw **one straight line** to separate the 1s from the 0s, and no straight line separates XOR's outputs. | 2 |
| 3a | One BPE algorithm handles "English, Japanese, Python code... emojis" with no language-specific rules | True only for **byte-level** BPE, which starts from raw bytes. Character-level BPE (ours) can't encode characters it never saw. Also, "handles" isn't "handles equally": languages that are rare in the training data get split into many more tokens, so they cost more and fit less into the context. | 3a |
| 1 | Word2vec pushes apart "every vocabulary word that never appears next to" a word | It uses **negative sampling**: a few *randomly sampled* words per step, not every unrelated word. Doing every word would be far too slow. | 3 |
| 2 | Cosine similarity of 0 means "completely unrelated", 1 means "identical" | 1 means *pointing the same direction* (not identical vectors), 0 means perpendicular, and **-1** means opposite. "Unrelated" is a loose interpretation. | 3 |
| 3b-i | The weights file is "a list of every word and the vector for that word" | That's what you *keep*, but training uses **two** vectors per word: one for a word as the centre and one for it as a neighbour. The second set is usually thrown away. | 3b |
| 3b-ii | Analogies like king - man + woman = queen "emerge" | They do emerge, but the published results **exclude the three input words** from the answers. Without that, the nearest word is often an input (we see this: "king"). Analogy results are also known to be less robust than the famous examples suggest. | 3b |
| 3b-iii | "Words with similar meanings end up as nearby points" | Words with similar **usage** end up nearby. Opposites like happy/sad appear in the same kinds of sentences, so they end up close too. | 3b |
| 3 | "BPE never merges across word boundaries" | That's a design choice made by GPT-style tokenizers (they split on a pattern first), not part of BPE itself. Some tokenizers treat spaces as ordinary characters. | 3 |
| 4 | Positional embedding turns the prompt "into a list of every possible token" | Each token ID is looked up to get a vector. A **position vector** is then *added* so the model knows word order. The "every possible token" list is only at the **output**. | 4 |
| 5 | Leaves out the **causal mask** | A text-generating transformer must stop each token from attending to tokens *after* it. Otherwise it cheats during training by looking at the answer. | 4 |
| 6 | Barely mentions residual connections and layer normalisation | "Add the input to the attention output" is a **residual connection**. Real blocks also use **layer normalisation**. Both are essential for stable training of deep stacks. | 4 |
| 6b | "Given that our model has an embedding size of 32 and six layers... 52,000 parameters" | Plausible, but the count depends heavily on **vocabulary size**. In our model the token embedding and output layer hold 56% of all parameters. Our 2-layer, 32-dim model has 59,324; at 6 layers it would have 109,756. | 4a |
| 6c | "We take the input matrix... add that to the attention matrix and then pass that through our feed-forward" | That's the **residual connection**. Our blocks (like GPT-2's) also apply **layer normalisation** before attention and before feed-forward, and the feed-forward's output is added back too. | 4a |
| 6d | Trains on "a little over 30 short children's stories" and shows the model continuing "once upon a time a small..." | The video never mentions a **validation set**. With about 30 stories, a model can simply memorise. Our overfitted model writes perfect prose that is a 48-word **verbatim copy** of a training story. Fluent output from a tiny dataset is more likely recitation than learning. | 4b |
| 6e | Training on a MacBook CPU "took over an hour and a half" | Training time depends on model size, steps and settings, not just "CPU vs GPU". Our 35k-parameter model trains in about 20 seconds, and longer training on this little data mostly makes the model *worse* (overfitting). | 4b |
| 7 | "Early layers syntax, middle meaning, late layers reasoning" | A rough tendency seen in some research, not a rule. Layers don't have such clean jobs. | 4 |
| 8 | The transformer's output "is a probability distribution" | It outputs **logits** (raw scores). Softmax turns them into probabilities. The video corrects this a minute later. | 5 |
| 9 | Top-p 0.2 means "only the top 20% of tokens are considered" | Top-p keeps the **smallest set of most-likely tokens whose probabilities add up to at least 0.2**. That could be 1 token or 500. It's about probability mass, not a percentage of the vocabulary. | 5 |
| 10 | Temperature 0 gives the same token every time | True in practice, but mathematically you can't divide by zero. Libraries treat 0 as a special case: "always pick the most likely token" (**greedy decoding**). | 5 |
| 10b | "A higher value like 1.5 will be much more random and sometimes incoherent" | Correct, and measurable. In our model, real words fall from 82% (greedy) to 53% at T=1.5. But low temperature isn't "better" either: greedy gave the same text 10 times out of 10 and got stuck in loops. | 5a |
| 10c | The context window "is all the transformer can see" | Correct, and the cropping is done by the **application**, not the model. Our model raises an error if given more than 32 tokens; the generation code decides to keep the newest ones. Products choose what to drop (old messages, the middle of documents...), and the system prompt must be protected from cropping. | 5a |
| 11 | Long chats degrade because "later messages have more weight in attention" | That's speculation. Documented effects include models using the *middle* of long contexts poorly, important instructions being diluted, and content being truncated. The causes vary by model. | 5 |
| 11b | The model sees "the system prompt... hidden instructions written by the AI provider" | Correct, but a system prompt only *influences* a model that was fine-tuned to follow instructions. Our pretrained-only model ignores it completely ("never" and "always" gave the same results). Even tuned models follow it often, not always, so rules that must always hold belong in application code. | 5b |
| 12 | RLHF is "humans rank responses" | Humans rank responses. Those rankings train a separate **reward model**, and the LLM is then optimised against it (or tuned directly on preference pairs, as in DPO). Some labs use AI feedback as well as, or instead of, human feedback. | 6 |
| 12b | Fine-tuning on question-answer pairs makes the model "give us more of a chatbot-like response" | It teaches **format and style** much more than facts. Our fine-tuned model answers "About the moon." with "The rold could talk.": perfect format, made-up content. Fine-tuning also costs earlier skills (forgetting), and the grading is usually masked so only the answer counts. | 6a |
| 12c | RLHF ranks responses as "good chatbot-like" vs bad | Preferences encode what raters **like**, not what's **true**. Optimising them can game the metric: our preference accuracy went from 0% to 100% while answers became "The rapy.". Real systems keep the tuned model close to the original (our `beta`) for exactly this reason. | 6a |
| 12d | The harness "can in turn actually do what the tool call said to do" | Correct about who acts, but a harness shouldn't simply do what it's told. It must validate the call and check permissions first, because the model can be wrong or fooled. Getting a tool result also doesn't guarantee a right answer: our model sometimes garbled a correct result. | 6b |
| 13 | LLMs hallucinate because of long context | Hallucination happens at any length. The model produces *plausible* text, and nothing in next-token prediction checks facts. | 5-6 |
